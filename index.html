03 Mid-Market operating model Context + challenge

Why Mid-Market and what was hard

Why I chose this example

No stable SOT, numerous asks without a stack rank, and leaders pulled into blockers weekly.

This was not a delivery problem. It was an operating-model problem—closely mirroring Kaseya’s unified-platform challenge: independent organizations, competing roadmaps, uneven technical readiness, and shared dependencies that require one execution system without direct authority.

The planning reality

  • 70–90+ requests with blank fields, no stack rank, and no frozen source of truth
  • A commitment requested before technical alignment
  • Feature-level ticket explosion with conditional accepts and weak follow-through
  • Leadership pulled into blockers at roughly one escalation every week

Why I was brought in

Lead SPOC across Business Platform and Mid-Market.

I owned intake, escalations, leadership communications, portfolio status, and TPM assignments—while driving cutline negotiation and request discipline with the core team.

03 Mid-Market operating model The operating system

Prioritize. Shift left. Assign E2E ownership. Run the hotspots.

01

Stack-rank the portfolio

Created the first stack-ranked list and negotiated a realistic cutline.

02

Shift alignment left

Required technical deep dives and designs before acceptance: workshops → requests → cutline.

03

Organize by capability

Reframed ticket sprawl into 3–6 product tracks, one request per feature, and reuse over rebuild.

04

Name E2E owners

Assigned a TPM POC per feature group, with each TPM owning Business Platform execution end to end.

05

Run one leadership system

Connected the Evergreen Deck, hotspot reviews, program review, and Super Scrum into one escalation path.

04 Kaseya Unified Platform Anticipated risks and challenges

Surface risks early.

I have encountered these risks in different forms—and know how to surface and resolve them early.

01 · Alignment

Product roadmaps compete with platform work

Early signal · Conditional commitments, slipping owners, local priorities overriding shared milestones.

Intervention · One stack rank, explicit capacity tradeoffs, named decision owners, and executive closure dates.

02 · Foundation

API maturity varies by product

Early signal · Unversioned endpoints, missing operations, undocumented behavior, or brittle adapters.

Intervention · Readiness heatmap, thin-slice validation, modernization owners, and minimum onboarding gates.

03 · Trust

Identity and authorization are inconsistent

Early signal · Permissions cannot be propagated or enforced consistently across tenant boundaries.

Intervention · Treat identity, authorization, isolation, and auditability as launch-critical—not fast follows.

04 · Data

Cross-product answers conflict

Early signal · Entities cannot be correlated, semantics differ, or data freshness is unknown.

Intervention · Contract tests, canonical mappings, lineage, quality thresholds, and accountable data owners.

05 · Scale

Onboarding demand outruns the foundation

Early signal · One-off integrations multiply while common capabilities remain unfinished.

Intervention · Balance customer value, readiness, reuse, risk, and capacity when sequencing every wave.

06 · Operations

The happy path masks production risk

Early signal · Missing SLOs, tracing, rate limits, degraded modes, or incident ownership.

Intervention · Production-readiness gates, failure testing, controlled rollout, kill switches, and exit criteria.

My role as Principal TPMTurn hidden assumptions into owned risks, time-bound decisions, and measurable readiness.
